Six setups, many uses

The practice needs photographs for the website, images for grant applications, plates for the annual book, and footage for video and teaching. That sounds like four jobs. It is six setups, and most of them feed more than one destination at once. Filter by what you need and the list shrinks to the rigs that actually produce it.

Choose a setup. Each opens what the rig is, how it is built, everything it feeds, and what tends to go wrong with it.

The one rule

Whatever is built has to live set up. A rig that needs assembling will not get used, and a documentation session that begins with twenty minutes of construction will quietly stop happening by March.

The front room has bookshelves on the far wall, two desks, and one window. One desk stays a making desk. The other becomes the documentation position and stays that way. Five of the six setups below share that corner.

Why window light and panel light are separated

Two of these setups need to be repeatable month after month and the rest do not, and that difference decides the lighting.

The record shot has to match across a year of releases and has to match the object that arrives in the post. Daylight in Surrey in November is not daylight in July, so that setup runs on panels with the curtains closed. Everything else can take the window, because a presentation photograph only has to be good rather than identical to the one taken six months ago.

The order to build them in

The flat copy stand first, because the studies release, the catalogue and the annual book all depend on it and none of them can proceed without it. The overhead slab second, because it is the cheapest presentation shot and it suits work on paper directly. Everything else after that, and only once the two before it are routine.

What has not been tested

Whether flat work propped against a sweep reads well or looks awkward is still open. The reference photography that shaped these setups is all of ceramics, and a pot solves the problem of standing up on its own. A panel does not.

Whether work on paper is photographed or scanned for the record shot is also still open. Scanning is more accurate and slower.
